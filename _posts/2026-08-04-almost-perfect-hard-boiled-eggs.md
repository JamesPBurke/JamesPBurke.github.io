A passing comment in a YouTube video introduced me to near-perfect hard-boiled eggs, and it's the method I plan to use for a while. 

The perfect hard boiled egg is elusive. I don't just mean getting the yolk cooked right, which is obviously an important part of the process. There are a lot of guides that will tell you how long to cook your eggs in boiling water at sea level to get a particular yolk doneness. Maybe they specify starting with cold water. Maybe they specify starting with boiling water. But, generally, they focus on the yolk because it's surrounded by the white part. That means that, when is cooked in a liquid at 212F (100c) (the temperature at sea-level atmospheric pressure of water while it is boiling), the heat has to pass through the white to get to the yolk, so there is a predictable relationship between how cooked the white is when the yolk gets to the target stage. 

You can think of the yolk-cooking and white-coming processes as two separate curves. Sorry to you folks who might be visualization challenged. You have to picture it in your head, as I don't have accurate graphics for this. Imagine that in these two separate graphs, there is a time when the white reaches perfect doneness, and there is a time (range) of acceptable yolk doneness. Your only control of this process when boiling an egg is to remove the egg at some time. The temperature of boiling water is a constant (unless you change elevation during the cooking process). So, you take the eggs out of the water, making an acceptable compromise, even if part of the egg is not cooked to your preference.

That's one thing that leads me to like slightly jammy eggs -- that yolk stage where it doesn't run at all, but it hasn't turned a chalky yellow. It may, instead, be a brighter, slightly orange gel. This is desirable (to me, at least) because I prefer that yolk to be more savory and flavorful.

But let's get back to talking about the egg whites. When the yolk is jammy in a boiled egg, the whites were cooked less because the whole thing was cooked less. And, in my opinion, that's a good thing. Because an overcooked white does several unpleasant things to an egg.

* It can make the white rubbery
* It can increase unpleasant sulphurous odors and flavors 
* It can begin to react with the yolk, turning the outer boundary an unpleasant gray color. 

And I've way to solve this whole deal is to just cook the egg less and enjoy your jammy egg. That's my "cooking compromise": an egg cooked the way I like but maybe not one my other family members might like. That's no good if you want to make a bunch of eggs that you can share. A better compromise would be an egg everyone likes. To change the dynamic between cooking the proteins in the white and the magical combination of flavors in the yolk, you have to move away from using boiling water. 

Boiling water as the default sets the relationship, because the set temperature means that everything cooks on a particular, familiar curve that puts the yolk and white on a timer, and your only choice is to take them out and stop the timer, even if one or the other aren't cooked to your liking. This exact problem is one that has been solved (kind of) with sous vide cooking. 

I'll refer you to elsewhere for the details of sous vide cooking other than that it adds temperature into the set of variables you have to pay with when cooking protein.
