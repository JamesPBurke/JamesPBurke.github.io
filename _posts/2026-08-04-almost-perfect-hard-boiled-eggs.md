A passing comment in a YouTube video introduced me to near-perfect hard-boiled eggs, and it's the method I plan to use for a while. 

The perfect hard boiled egg is elusive. I don't just mean getting the yolk cooked right, which is obviously an important part of the process. There are a lot of guides that will tell you how long to cook your eggs in boiling water at sea level to get a particular yolk doneness. Maybe they specify starting with cold water. Maybe they specify starting with boiling water. But, generally, they focus on the yolk because it's surrounded by the white part. That means that, when is cooked in a liquid at 212F (100c), the heat has to pass through the white to get to the yolk, so there is a predictable relationship between how cooked the white is when the yolk gets to the target stage. 

That's one thing that leads me to like slightly jammy eggs -- that yolk stage where it doesn't run at all, but it hasn't turned a chalky yellow. It may, instead, be a brighter, slightly orange gel. This is desirable (to me, at least) because I prescribe that yolk to be more savory and flavorful. 

But we were talking about the egg whites. When the yolk is jammy in a boiled egg, the whites were cooked less because the whole thing was cooked less. And, in my opinion, that's a good thing. Because an overcooked white does several unpleasant things to an egg.

* It can make the white rubbery
* It can increase unpleasant sulphurous odors and flavors 
* It can begin to react with the yolk, turning the outer boundary an unpleasant gray color. 

And I've way to solve this whole deal is to just cook the egg less and enjoy your jammy egg.

But there are reasons to fully cook your egg. Among them: a spouse who likes them more cooked and someone wants to make egg salad or deviled eggs.
