# How This Started

A video I linked below gives one of the most clear and supported distinctions between Zach Snyder's adaptations of comics vs. James Gunn's (the latter of which I much prefer). The thesis is that while it's possible to translate the comics by precisely capturing the visual style and dramatic moment (analogous to direct word-for-word translations of text), in order to convey a meaningful story in an adaptation (which necessarily reflects either the meaning in the head of the original author, the filmmaker's connection with the original stories, or both) an approach that centers on what defines the characters and how to get them where they need to go in the story[^connect].

[^connect]: By "get them where they need to go," I mean progressing the characters through their growth arc, or revealing the character's nature. Or whatever else characters are for -- I'm not a literary dude.]

But I want to talk about something that comes up in the video, which I'm going to call "The Rorschach Problem."

# The Homelander Problem

## Who is Homelander?

If you're not familiar with *The Boys*, which is a comic series I didn't read that got turned into an Amazon Prime Video series that many people, including me, watched, it is centered on the premise that superheroes are corporate sponsored and controlled properties in a world made cynical by violence, corruption, misogyny, and greed. This dystopian background is papered over with patriotism and action movies. The heroes are not only running around doing quasi-heroic things; they have contracts to do advertising and star as themselves in big budget blockbuster films which craft their mythologies. For many young people, these heroes eem like figures to admire, and they're only disillusioned if they come up against the corporatist government or any of the heroes in any meaningful way.

The absolutely worst example of the bunch is Homelander, brilliantly, yet ickily, played by Anthony Starr. In his dark blue super-suit, accompanied by red boots, gauntlets, and striped cape, he's less like an embodiment of the American flag (his cape actually is the flag) and more like a red, blue, and gold Aryan statue come to life. Starr's somewhat boyish features lend the character some of its sinister irony, as Homelander is a cruel, unsympathetic, cynical villain who quietly revels in the limelight that he enjoys and frequently exploits. 

## The Homelander Surprise

You likely wouldn't choose this character as a role model for your son. And yet, Homelander is also a deeply insecure and lonely individual, who must constantly appear perfect. For some of the series he is under the thumb of the Vaught corporation, but as a supremely powerful being with apparently no limits other than his crushing need for approval. Those flaws, along with the anti-establishment satire and power fantasy made him a compelling figure to many online communities.  

This took showrunner Eric Kripke by surprise (according to him)[^kripke] but I think there was a whole boatload of precedent for this kind of effect. And that's even if you don't take into account that the show made conscious references to today's politics early in the series as the writers attempted to portray a fascist/authoritarian creep over the seasons. And that they used clear references to the boogeymen of our own alt-right as victims of people like Homelander in a way that likely sent a strong message that "this guy is you" while failing to convey that this was a **Bad Thing**. Just because Homelander laser-eyes a woke mob to death doesn't make people desensitized to cartoonish entertainment media violence reflect on their own personalities.

[^kripke]: I'm not sure I completely buy his surprise. By 2024 he was saying that he had given up trying to understand [Homelander's appeal.](https://www.slashfilm.com/1599634/the-boys-showrunner-eric-kripke-responds-to-homelander-fans/) I described his personality and flaws above. And, again, on top of that, he made connections to today's cultural backlash against (for lack of a better set of words that are not already appropriated and twisted) personal and institutionalized compassion for others and empathy for those with different lives and different choices. # The Rorschach Problem

## The Cynical Element

The reason I say Kripke should have seen it coming is that he must be familiar with the celebrated comic min-series *Watchmen* by Allen Moore that *The Boys* owes quite a bit to in theme, if not in style or substance. 

In the universe of *Watchmen*, costumed vigilantes have been around for decades in the US, and they're all rather disturbed individuals. At the very least, they're getting off on violence, but the worst are (here's that word again) cynical, hyper-violent, rapists, and mass-murderers. There's nothing there to identify with, right?

*Watchmen* came out in the 80's, when Gen X was probably at the hight of its post-adolescent cynicism come-down. Or, at least, it probably ought to have been if the generation had been on track to mature a bit. In reality, cynicism was still the cool alternative to what I perceived as the optimism of previous decades. I can really only tell you how I experienced it -- a lot of it through media. There was some sort of simplicity to old music of the 40's and TV of the 50's, which were as much a part of my life in the 60's and 70's as The Office, Game of Thrones, Mad Men, The Big Bang Theory, Breaking Bad, and The Sopranos are on today's popular culture[^memes]. 

I don't think Gen X[^genx] believed in the simplicity and apparent optimism. I can tell you that disco music struck me as extremely peppy. The desire to move to disco is infectious. I was unaware of the culture surrounding it, but I knew that sort of thing made you feel good. When more broody music came around, it intrigued me. And the electronic precision that many artists were experimenting with reminded me of Bach, which had a kind of mechanical complexity that I found compelling in a way that drumbeats in disco didn't. Later, I was fascinated by the anger under the surface in some punk and punk-adjacent music. And, like young people often mistakenly do, I not only embraced some music, but I rejected other music in my own claim to an identity.

This isn't meant to be a music history. I know that a lot was going on in music that I was unaware of. The short version is that I am familiar with a young person's rejection of previous generations. And that for me, maybe for my generation, there was a cynical element. We were facing possible nuclear annihilation. That was our probably future. You can talk about today's youth and the despair that America no longer affords the same economic opportunities that previous generations used to grow their lives, but the version we faced was: you're probably going to die in a nuclear war, but maybe if you work hard you can be some kind of success before you're vaporized."

As I write this, I realize I'm trying to excuse something that's coming up.

## Who is Rorschach?

Rorschach is a character in Watchmen who is a monster of a particular type. He's a deranged and broken conspiracy theorist, the product of an abusive childhood and some unsavory experiences. He's cynical. But he's relentless, observant, wiry, and possessing of a complete moral clarity regarding his actions whether they lead him to violence or to justice. 

Alan Moore created Rorschach (so-named for the ever-shifting symmetrical blotch on his mysteriously-functioning stocking mask) as a sort of examination of Batman, the relentless dark knight detective who is driven to inhuman extremes of vigilantism by the trauma of watching his parents murdered before his eyes. Some of the details are tweaked (the fortune, the no-kill policy) but at its core, Rorschach is a commentary on (spoiler alert) Bruce Wayne's character. Because all the folks in *Watchmen* have something wrong with them. Because the whole idea of masked vigilantism is nuts in a slightly-more-realistic world.
