# Spoiler

To spoil this whole post, I'm sorry that I'm going to not be doing some things that I would otherwise do. I'm pretty sure you'll forgive me. But if you want to understand what I mean, there's this post.

# Memories

Old social media photos from our 2016 trip to Europe cropped up in FB Memories, and I was shocked when I saw a photo Maggie took of me on the balcony. Ten years ago, and I was just about in the best shape I'd ever been in. Better than when I ran my first half marathon. Better than when I was lighter, and younger, because I had more cardiovascular endurance and strength.

Then I saw a photo of the year I was hired to teach. Still in OK shape. I'd lost weight over the summer before I started -- the summer of 2019. I was trying low carb diet that was ultimately not for me, but worth the experience while I was teaching a graduate course (Technology for Math Teachers) at UMass Dartmouth. I had gained a little bit during my Post Doc.  Injuries led me to ruin less, and I had learned to eat like a person who ran 30 miles a week.

Something mysterious happened between getting hired and June of 2026.

# Weight, Weight, Don't Tell Me

Weight is not necessarily an indicator of health. Let me get that out there.

But it's also not ***not*** an indicator, if you understand me. And if you understand it. It's highly personal. Not being a physician, I'll just say this: weight can be a visible puzzle piece in a person's health picture[^health-puzzle]. In my case, I'm a stress-eater. For valid, personal, medical reasons, my doctor has repeatedly suggested I lose weight over the last two years. That's not a wild request. It turns out that I'm gaining more than 5lb every year that I've been teaching high school. Consider that indicator a puzzle piece, but with its own possible consequences that are **no bueno**.


[^health-puzzle]: If you suddenly lose a lot of weight, your doctor might ask you what's changed in your behavior, because that is a change, and he wants to know whether what he's seeing is unexplained. Same thing for weight gain. It raises questions for the person concerned with your health. Casually, people may ask you questions as well, but that is not a professional interaction. And there are secondary effects of weight gain or loss. There are disorders that become more likely if you go too far in one direction or another. And there are psychological conditions worth being concerned about when a drastic change happens. Weight is not health, but there are many examples of it as a puzzle piece as well as being able to change your health outlook.

So, yeah, 5lbs a year adds up. Over 7 years, that's 35lbs. **That's** no bueno.

I'm a stress eater. And a teacher.

This post isn't about being a teacher; It's about my cycle of stress and an unhealthy escape hatch. 

I'm interested in just about everything. I take on things. And I don't like to fail. But paralysis of various times can make you fail. And being overwhelmed is like a cliff that you can't see coming; it's a feeling. It doesn't happen at some quantitatively measurable point. It manifests just by thinking. You can have a bunch of things you need to do, and you're holding them in your head in some mysterious back room of your brain. And a few pop out that you know you haven't put into some smart organizing system -- like a notebook -- and suddenly you're overwhelmed. And the worst part is that it can cause paralysis, which can eventually make you fail. Yuck. 

This was really bad in middle school and high school. But much later I learned that if you're always overwhelmed, there's a way you can deal with it -- knowing you'll never catch up. And some jobs are like that. And I guess some people find ways to deal with that. I found one reliable way: eating.

I love eating, thinking about eating, thinking about making food to eat, and all the related things. Making food for other people, thinking about what people might like to eat, thinking about what other people do eat, thinking about the things I don't know about cooking and eating. And -- fun fact -- there's this cool thing you can enjoy while you're thinking about food. And that's eating it! And it's a nice coincidence that you have to eat anyway, so it's like if doing the laundry was your hobby.

Briefly, I tried a weight-loss program that charged $70/month and aimed to teach you about your own psychology. And it wasn't bad, but after the first $140 or so, I realized that their suggestions weren't all that helpful. And that I already knew I was a stress eater. I guess they forced me to think about it more seriously. But it didn't get me to stop eating.

To cut to the chase: I learned that when I'm easily distracted by being overwhelmed, eating while I'm trying to focus helps by turning something off. It's not just turning off hunger. It's moe complicated than that. But it works. Until you're 40lbs heavier and your doctor is putting you on blood pressure medication. 

I have ways of wrestling with the eating problem, and they work great. That's the *output* side of this equation: controlling my eating habits, and establishing new habits. But the pressure that is pushing me is the *input*
